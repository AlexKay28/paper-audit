# Scientific Storytelling & Narrative Structure

**Sources:** compiled from the works listed in §8 below (Schimel, Olson,
Carpenter/The Open Notebook, Truby, McKee).
**Compiled:** Aug 20, 2026

Narrative is not decoration layered onto a finished paper — it is the structure
that determines whether a reader can reconstruct the argument. Popular science
and academic writing both rely on narrative, but they optimize for different
things: pop science hooks the reader through curiosity and relatable framing;
academic writing prioritizes clarity, cognitive efficiency, and logical
precision. The mechanics below apply to both, with the mode-specific
differences set out in §1.

> Attribution note: the claims in this file are the positions of the cited
> authors, not independently established findings. Where a source asserts an
> empirical effect (e.g. Schimel's claim that narratively structured papers are
> cited and funded more), it is marked as that author's claim.

## 1. Two modes: sci-pop vs. academic

| Technique | Popular science / sci-pop | Academic literature & papers |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook / opening** | **Narrative lede:** a character, a dramatic experiment, or a mind-bending question. | **Contextual gap:** what is known, what is unknown, and why that gap matters. |
| **Pacing engine** | **Human struggle:** the process of discovery, failed hypotheses, breakthrough moments. | **Logical flow:** clear linkage between problem, methodology, and empirical evidence. |
| **Language** | Concrete analogies, sensory detail, active voice. | Precise terminology, unambiguous definitions, controlled scope. |
| **Core goal** | Illuminate significance and trigger wonder. | Transfer verifiable knowledge with absolute clarity. |

For a research paper the academic column governs. The sci-pop column still
supplies usable technique — analogy, concreteness, active voice, an explicit
surprise — but it is subordinate to precision. Narrative devices must never
manufacture significance the evidence does not support (see `04` §1, §6.1 on
speculation dressed as explanation).

## 2. The narrative arc of a paper (Schimel, *Writing Science*)

Schimel's core argument: formal research papers already follow narrative arcs,
and IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion) is best read as a
storytelling framework that leads a reviewer seamlessly through one thesis.

**OCAR** — the arc of a whole paper:

- **O**pening — the context a reader needs before the specific problem exists.
  Establishes the world of the paper.
- **C**hallenge — the knowledge gap, contradiction, or unmet need. This is the
  question the paper exists to answer.
- **A**ction — what was done: methods, experiments, analysis.
- **R**esolution — the answer to the challenge, and what it changes.

Key structural consequences:

- The Resolution must resolve the *stated* Challenge. A discussion that lands on
  a different takeaway than the one the introduction promised breaks the arc.
- The abstract is the arc in miniature: context → gap → approach → finding →
  meaning.
- A title that states a finding ("X improves Y under Z") carries more of the arc
  than a bare topic label ("On X and Y").
- **Stress position:** readers retain what sits at the *end* of a sentence,
  paragraph, and section. Put the important item there, not in the middle.
- One paragraph, one point — stated in the paragraph's first or last sentence.
- The figure sequence should carry the argument on its own: a reader flipping
  through only the figures should be able to follow the story.

Schimel's claim: papers with clear narrative structure are cited more and
attract more funding. Treat this as the book's thesis rather than a settled
empirical result.

## 3. The ABT framework (Olson, *Houston, We Have a Narrative*)

A narrative template for any explanation, from a single paragraph to a whole
paper:

- **And** — the baseline context. What is stably true.
- **But** — the problem, anomaly, or contradiction that disrupts it.
- **Therefore** — the discovery, response, or consequence.

Why it works: it forces every technical explanation into a problem-solving arc
and prevents **"and-then" writing** — a boring list of facts connected only by
sequence, with no cause-and-effect. "We did A, and then B, and then C" tells the
reader nothing about why any step was necessary.

Practical tests:

- Can the paper's core argument be written as one ABT sentence?
- Does every major section transition explain why the next step *follows*?
- Is there exactly **one** central "But"? Multiple competing conflicts fragment
  the narrative and leave the reader unsure which question the paper answers.

## 4. Information laddering & scaffolded complexity

Introduce a complex concept in three distinct layers:

1. A visceral metaphor or analogy.
2. A simplified conceptual baseline (the idea with the details suppressed).
3. The precise technical treatment.

Why it works: a non-expert reader needs a mental anchor before dense mechanics,
or they hit cognitive overload and stop reconstructing the argument.

Practical consequences:

- Give a one-paragraph version of the method before the full formal treatment.
- Ground abstract machinery in a concrete running example or toy case.
- State where each analogy **breaks down**. An analogy without stated limits
  becomes a false model the reader carries into the rest of the paper.
- Keep terminology density low on the opening page — every undefined term spent
  there is borrowed against the reader's attention.

## 5. Epistemic curiosity gaps (D-type vs. I-type)

Two kinds of curiosity are usually distinguished: **interest-type (I-type)**,
the pleasant pull of something new, and **deprivation-type (D-type)**, the
uncomfortable need to close a specific gap in one's own understanding.

D-type is the stronger engine, and it is activated by a **contradiction**: a gap
between what the reader thinks they understand and how the world actually
behaves. Highlight the paradox, the failed expectation, or the result that
should not have happened.

Constraints for a research paper:

- Frame results against the reader's likely prior expectation, so the surprise
  is legible as surprise.
- Make the counterintuitive element explicit rather than burying it in a table.
- **Pay off every gap you open.** A hook the results never address is a
  rhetorical debt, and it reads to a reviewer as overclaiming (`04` §1).

## 6. Craft: prose-level mechanics (Carpenter, *The Craft of Science Writing*)

A playbook for turning research into narrative without losing precision:

- Find the concrete element — a specific case, system, failure, or decision —
  and let it carry the abstraction.
- Prefer active voice with the actor named where agency matters; passive voice
  hides who did what and to which data.
- Balance precision against engagement deliberately: engagement is what buys the
  reader's attention, precision is what the paper is *for*. When they conflict
  in a research paper, precision wins.
- Use jargon only where it buys precision. Jargon deployed as a marker of rigor
  is the language-misuse failure mode of `04` §3–4.

## 7. General story foundations

- **Truby, *The Anatomy of Story*** — 22-step organic structure; plot driven by
  a protagonist's flaw and psychological need rather than by event sequence.
- **McKee, *Story*** — narrative value shifts, scene design, and the tension
  between a protagonist's desire and the forces opposing it.

For scientific writing the transferable core is: a narrative advances when a
*value changes* under opposition. In a paper, the opposing force is the
alternative explanation, the baseline that already works, or the confound — and
the value that shifts is what the field can now claim to know.
